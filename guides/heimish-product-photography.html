Photographing food, seforim and judaica for your website

Bad product photos read as a store nobody trusts. How to shoot food, seforim and packaged goods with a phone, a window and $60 of gear — and size the files right.

Heimish business web

By BH Web Solutions · Updated 2026-07-29

On a website, the photo is the product. Nobody can pick up the container, turn the sefer over, or read the hechsher through the glass. Everything the customer would normally do with their hands has to happen in a picture, and that is why photography is usually the highest-return afternoon of work in the whole project.

Why the pictures decide whether the store sells

Two things happen at once when someone lands on a product page. The first is information: a shopper who cannot tell whether that is a nine-inch or a twelve-inch pan, or whether that Mishnah Berurah is the large-type edition, does not guess. They leave. The second is trust, and it is the bigger one. A dark, tilted, yellow photo taken next to the register does not read as "small business." It reads as abandoned — the site is old, the stock is stale, the order might not arrive. That judgment happens in about a second, and good copy further down the page will not undo it.

This is especially unfair to heimish businesses, because so many are genuinely excellent and genuinely old — a butcher with forty years of customers, a seforim store people drive an hour to reach. None of that reputation travels through a bad photograph. Online you start over.

The bar is not "magazine quality." The bar is: clear, bright, honest, and the same as the one next to it. That is achievable with a phone in an afternoon. Chasing more than that is where people stall out and end up shipping nothing.

The kit you actually need

A phone is genuinely fine. Any iPhone or Android from roughly the last five years has a main camera that outresolves anything a website needs. Professional cameras buy low-light performance and shallow depth of field, neither of which matters when you are shooting a jar of pickles beside a window. What you do need:

  • A window. Free. Ideally large, without direct sun coming through it at the hour you shoot. In the northern hemisphere a north-facing window gives soft, steady light all day, which is why it has been the standard for still-life painting for centuries.
  • A sweep. White or light grey poster board, curved up the wall behind the product rather than creased, so there is no visible line where table meets wall. Two or three dollars. A roll of seamless paper is about $30 and lasts a year.
  • A reflector. White foam board propped on the shadow side, bouncing window light back into the dark half of the product. Five dollars, and it does more than any camera upgrade you could buy.
  • Something to hold the phone still. A $20 tripod with a phone clamp — not for shake, for repeatability. If the phone does not move between products, every photo in the catalog lines up.
  • Tape, museum putty, small blocks. For propping labels forward and stopping cans from rolling.

Total: under $60, most of it reusable forever. If somebody quotes you thousands for a lightbox rig before you have ever shot a test frame, shoot the test frame first.

What to skip

Skip the phone's built-in flash — it flattens everything and throws a white blob onto any glossy package. Skip Portrait mode; the artificial background blur mangles edges, especially on anything with a handle or a spout, and it ruins the image for later cutouts. Skip the ultrawide and the digital zoom, which are softer than the main lens. And skip filters: one that makes a single photo pop makes a catalog look incoherent.

Light is the whole thing

Why store lighting ruins product photos

The most common mistake is shooting the product where it sits — on the shelf, under the overhead fixtures. Three problems stack up.

Color. Fluorescent tubes and cheap LED panels do not emit a full spectrum. They are rated by CRI, the Color Rendering Index, on a scale where daylight is 100; inexpensive tubes commonly land in the 60s and 70s, and what is missing is disproportionately the deep reds. Beef and lamb get their color from myoglobin, a pigment that reads bright red in daylight — under a light that barely emits red, it goes brown-grey. The meat is fine. The photo is not. Display cases are often fitted with lamps chosen specifically to render red well, which is why the meat looks better in the case than in your picture of the case.

Direction. Overhead light comes straight down. It puts a bright cap on the top of every package, drops the label — the part the customer needs to read — into shadow, and carves a hard shadow directly under the product.

Glare. Small, hard sources reflect off cellophane, shrink wrap, foil lids and laminated labels as blinding hotspots. A large soft source like a window reflects as a broad, gentle sheen you can position out of the way.

There is also a mechanical annoyance: US mains power cycles at 60 Hz, so many fluorescent and cheap LED fixtures pulse 120 times a second, and phone cameras sometimes catch that as faint horizontal banding. If you see stripes in your photos, that is what it is, and editing does not fix it properly.

The window setup

Put a small table beside the window, not in front of it, so light crosses the product from the side. Clip the sweep so it curves up the wall, set the product about two feet from the glass, put the foam board the same distance away on the far side, and watch what happens as you slide the board in and out. The shadows lift. That is the entire lighting lesson. If direct sun is hitting the window, tape a white bedsheet or tracing paper over it — direct sun is a small hard source, and through a sheet it becomes a big soft one.

Then lock the exposure and the color. On an iPhone, tap and hold on the product until AE/AF LOCK appears; Android cameras have an equivalent. This stops the phone re-guessing brightness and color for every item, which is the number one cause of a catalog where one yogurt is warm and the next is blue.

Food that looks fresh, not styled

Commercial food styling exists to make food survive hours under hot lights: glue instead of milk, motor oil for syrup, meat undercooked and torched. None of that belongs in your store, because the customer receives the actual item and compares. Overselling a kugel is worse than underselling it — the disappointment arrives at their door. What actually reads as fresh:

  • Moisture and texture. Gloss on a brisket, condensation on a cold salad, crumbs at the edge of a cake. Side light reveals texture; overhead light erases it.
  • Shoot cold or shoot fast. Hot food dies in about ninety seconds — sheen goes matte, greens dull, cheese sets. Have the frame and focus set before the food reaches the table.
  • Real portions in real packaging. If you deliver in an aluminum half-pan, photograph the half-pan. A caterer who shows a beautifully plated single serving of something sold by the tray is generating phone calls, not orders.
  • Angles by food type. Roughly 45 degrees — the angle of someone sitting down — for plated food and anything with height. Straight overhead for flat things: platters, spreads, a table of trays. Straight on at product height for packages and bottles. Pick per category, then never deviate inside it.
  • Backgrounds that don't fight. Neutral wood, white, grey, slate. Never a red or pink surface behind raw meat; it drags the meat toward grey by contrast.

Raw meat is the hardest subject here and there is no trick for it. Get it out of the case, onto clean white or butcher paper, near the window, and shoot within a minute or two before the surface oxidizes. Do not "fix" it afterward by pushing saturation until it glows — beyond looking fake, that is a picture misrepresenting what you sell.

Seforim: the hardest category, and the one worth the effort

One sefer cover contains the two hardest things a camera can face at once: a very dark, often textured leather field, and a mirror-bright gold-stamped title on top of it. Left alone the phone does one of two things — brightens for the leather so the gold blows out into a white smear, or protects the gold so the cover becomes a muddy black rectangle with no visible embossing.

Gold stamping and dark leather

Gilt lettering is a mirror. A mirror does not "look gold"; it shows you whatever it is pointed at. So the fix is not more light, it is aiming the reflection.

  • Use a large, soft source — window light, or window light through a sheet — never a bare bulb or the flash. A big source gives the gold a broad gradient to reflect instead of a white dot.
  • Tilt the sefer, not the camera. Lay it flat or on a small stand and rock it a few degrees at a time while watching the screen. There is a narrow angle at which the gold suddenly reads as warm metal with depth, and you find it by moving, not by editing.
  • Put the white reflector on the dark side, to raise the leather without touching the gold.
  • Expose slightly for the gold — a touch darker than the phone wants — then lift the shadows afterward. Detail hiding in a dark area is recoverable. Detail burned to pure white is gone permanently.
  • Black leather against a pure white sweep is a fight. Light grey or warm off-white keeps both ends of the range in play and looks more considered anyway.

Shoot the spine and the cover together

This is the single most useful thing a seforim store can do, and almost nobody does it. Seforim buyers are not buying a title; they are buying a specific edition, publisher and printing. The spine is where that lives, along with the volume number and the set it belongs to. A cover-only photo forces a phone call. A cover-and-spine photo closes the sale.

Stand the sefer up, turned maybe 20 to 30 degrees so you get the full front and a foreshortened spine in one frame — and note that a Hebrew book opens the other way, so with the cover facing you, the spine sits on the right side of the frame, not the left. Compose it out of habit like an English book and you produce a catalog of seforim that look subtly wrong to every customer you have.

For a set, shoot the full row of spines as a second image. For anything with a distinctive layout — large type, wide margins, a text with or without nekudos — add one open-page photo. Those three images answer nearly every question a seforim buyer has.

Keeping the Hebrew legible

Hebrew title text has finer strokes than most Latin type and is frequently stamped rather than printed, so it is already fighting for contrast against its own background.

  • Get the book square to the camera. Keystoning — the top narrower than the bottom because you shot down at it — damages Hebrew letterforms far more than English ones.
  • Never crop the title. Whatever your standard square crop is, the title must survive it.
  • Match the physical object. Spine stamping direction varies by publisher, so photograph it the way it reads when the sefer stands on your own shelf; do not rotate it for visual symmetry.
  • If the site itself runs in Hebrew or Yiddish, the page is right-to-left and image alignment and captions must follow — covered in the guide on Yiddish and Hebrew websites.

Packaged goods: the hechsher has to be readable

For a large share of your customers, the certification symbol is not a detail. It is the first thing they look for and sometimes the only thing that decides the purchase. It is also typically printed a quarter-inch wide in a corner of the package, which means it survives no crop and no compression.

The working answer is two images per packaged item: the main straight-on shot of the whole package, and a tight second shot of the hechsher panel — the symbol, and usually the pas yisroel, cholov yisroel or yoshon line beside it, at a size where it is unambiguous on a phone screen. Fifteen extra seconds per product removes an entire category of incoming phone calls.

Two cautions. Glare is the enemy here: foil and gloss under hard light will wipe out exactly the symbol you are trying to show, so angle the package until the reflection moves off it. And more seriously, the photo is a claim. If a supplier changes certification between runs, or you photographed the imported version and now stock the domestic one, the picture on your site is telling customers something untrue. Treat a new package like a new price: it means a new photo. New York also has a kosher labeling statute requiring sellers to disclose the certifying agency for products represented as kosher, enforced by the state Department of Agriculture and Markets, so this is not purely a courtesy issue.

Consistency beats any single great photo

If you take one thing from this page, take this. A hundred merely-decent photos sharing the same angle, background, lighting and crop look professional. Five stunning photos among ninety-five inconsistent ones look like a mess with five stolen images in it — and some customers will assume exactly that.

The reason is structural. On a category page products appear in a grid, six or twelve at a time, and the eye compares them to each other before reading any of them. Variation in background brightness or shadow direction registers as noise, and noise reads as disorder. So write the standard down before you start, and do not change it mid-catalog:

Decide onceTypical choice
BackgroundWhite or light grey sweep, same sheet all day
AngleStraight on for packages; 45° for prepared food; 20–30° for seforim
CropSquare (1:1), product occupying roughly 80–85% of the frame
Camera heightFixed on a tripod; move the product, not the phone
Shadow directionSame side for every shot — window stays where it is
Images per product1 main + 1 detail (hechsher panel, spine, or texture)

That 80–85% rule matters more than it sounds. If one product fills the frame and the next floats small in the middle, a shopper reads the second as smaller and cheaper. Keep the visual weight even and let the written dimensions do the sizing.

White background or lifestyle shot?

Both, for different jobs.

Plain white or light grey is for the catalog itself — the grid, search results, the product page main image. It shows the item without competition, crops predictably at any size, and sits cleanly on a white page. It is also required territory if you ever list on outside channels: Amazon requires a pure white background on main product images, and Google's Shopping feed rejects images carrying promotional text, watermarks, borders or "SALE" badges. Clean primary images can be fed anywhere later without a reshoot.

Lifestyle — food on a Shabbos table, a sefer open on a shtender, the platter as it will look when guests arrive — belongs on the homepage, category headers, the about page, and the second or third slot in a product gallery. It sells the idea. It does not show what arrives in the box, and it makes a poor thumbnail, because at 200 pixels a busy scene turns to mush. A caterer is the one case where lifestyle carries more weight, since what is really being sold is the finished table — but every individual tray still needs its own clean shot in the ordering list.

Getting the files right for the web

Why a 6MB photo is a problem

A photo straight out of a modern phone is roughly 4000 pixels wide and two to six megabytes, and it will be displayed at maybe 800 pixels. You are sending several times the data anyone can see. One such photo is nothing; a category page with twenty-four of them is 50 to 100 megabytes of images.

On a good home connection that is a few seconds of blank grey boxes. On a phone in a parking lot with two bars it can be thirty seconds, burning the customer's data plan to do it. People do not wait, and they rarely blame their connection — they blame your store. Images are almost always the largest thing on a store's pages, which makes this the highest-leverage speed fix available. More in the guide on site speed for online stores.

Sizes, formats and compression in plain terms

  • Resize before uploading. 1500 to 2000 pixels on the long edge is plenty — big enough that zoom stays sharp, small enough to be sane. Free tools on Mac and Windows batch-resize a whole folder in one action.
  • Aim for under about 200 KB per image after compression. For a photograph on a plain background that is usually indistinguishable from the original at normal viewing size.
  • Format. JPEG is the safe universal choice for photographs. WebP, which Google developed and reports as roughly 25–35% smaller than JPEG at comparable quality, is supported by every current browser; AVIF is smaller still and now broadly supported. Use PNG only for genuine transparency, since PNG photographs are enormous. WordPress has accepted WebP uploads since version 5.8 and added AVIF in 6.5, and most well-built sites convert automatically — so in practice you upload a JPEG and the site handles the rest.
  • Two iPhone gotchas. iPhones save in HEIC by default and plenty of systems still choke on it — if uploads fail mysteriously, set the camera format to "Most Compatible," which shoots JPEG. They also capture in a wide gamut (Display P3) while the web assumes sRGB, so colors that look punchy on the phone can shift on a desktop monitor. Converting to sRGB before upload avoids the surprise.
  • Name the files properly. chocolate-babka-16oz.jpg, not IMG_4471.jpg. It costs nothing, it helps image search, and in eighteen months it is the only reason anyone will find the original.

Alt text

Alt text is the short written description attached to an image. A blind customer's screen reader speaks it aloud, it appears if the image fails to load, and search engines read it. Under WCAG 2.1 — the accessibility standard US courts and regulators point to — a text alternative for non-text content is criterion 1.1.1, which sits at Level A, the most basic tier.

Write it as you would describe the item on the phone. "Chocolate babka, one pound, sliced, in a clear bakery box." "Mishnah Berurah volume 3, large-type edition, brown leather binding with gold lettering." Not "babka babka best babka Brooklyn kosher bakery." Keyword stuffing helps nothing and degrades the experience for the person the text exists for. If an image is purely decorative and the product name is already beside it, an empty alt attribute is correct — better than a screen reader saying the same thing twice. More in the guide on website accessibility and the ADA.

Shooting 100 products in an afternoon

Very doable — but only if you stop treating each product as its own photo session. Assume two people, four to five hours, two to three minutes an item once you are moving.

  1. Build the set once, in the morning. Window, table, sweep, tripod, reflector. Lock exposure and white balance on a grey or white card, and do not move the tripod again all day.
  2. Shoot one test product and look at it big, on a laptop rather than the phone. Glare, color casts and crooked labels all hide on a 6-inch screen. Whatever is wrong is about to be wrong ninety-nine more times.
  3. Sort into like groups first. Boxed goods, then jars, then bagged items, then seforim. Similar shapes need the same setup, so grouping removes nearly all re-adjustment.
  4. Use the SKU card trick. Before each product, hold a card showing its item number in front of the lens and take one frame. Every product now has a visible divider in front of it, and matching hundreds of images to hundreds of items three days later becomes a five-minute job instead of an evening.
  5. Two roles. One person shoots and never leaves the camera. The other stages: next item, wipe dust, label facing forward, previous item away. The photographer's rhythm is what you are protecting.
  6. Detail shots in the same beat. Main shot, step in for the hechsher or the spine, next product. Saving them for a second pass means handling every item twice.
  7. Batch the editing at the end. Crop, straighten and export as one operation across the folder. That is what produces consistency; doing it image by image is what destroys it.
  8. Keep the originals in a dated folder, backed up somewhere that is not the phone. You will want to re-crop for a new layout in two years.

A first day runs slower — call it sixty products — because you are learning the setup. The second session goes at nearly double the speed, and by the third you are adding new stock in ten-minute batches as it comes in. That is the real goal: not a heroic photo day, but a routine. Handing the job to a photographer is a perfectly reasonable choice too — the standard above is what to ask them for, and the file requirements are what to hand them. When we build a store, the images are usually the part that decides how well it performs.

The short version

  • Photos are the single biggest driver of whether an online store sells. A phone, a window, a white sweep and a $5 foam reflector are enough — well under $60 of equipment.
  • Never shoot under overhead store lighting. Low-CRI fluorescent light drains the red out of meat, drops labels into shadow, and puts hard glare on every glossy package.
  • Seforim need the gold tilted until it reads as metal rather than a white blowout, and they need a cover-and-spine pair so buyers can identify the edition — with the spine on the right, as a Hebrew book actually sits.
  • Photograph the hechsher as its own close-up, and reshoot when the packaging changes. The image is a claim about what you are selling.
  • Consistency across the catalog — same angle, background, crop, shadow direction — matters more than any single beautiful shot.
  • Resize to 1500–2000 pixels and under roughly 200 KB before uploading, write plain-English alt text for every product image, and keep the untouched originals backed up.
